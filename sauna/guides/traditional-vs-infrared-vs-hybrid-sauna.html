Traditional, infrared or hybrid — which sauna should you get?

The difference is not a specification. It is what happens to you when you sit down in one.

Dubai, UAE · Updated November 2026

A traditional sauna heats the air, and you feel the room. Infrared panels warm your body directly at a much lower air temperature. A hybrid cabin has both and lets you choose session by session.

Most comparisons stop at the temperatures. The decision is really about what you want the twenty minutes to feel like.

Traditional

This is the sauna people picture: a wood-lined room at 80–100°C, a heater topped with stones, a ladle and a bucket. You throw water on the stones, the room takes a breath, and the heat changes character for a minute. That moment — löyly, in Finnish — is the whole experience, and it is the thing infrared cannot do.

It is social in a way the others are not. It is also physically more demanding, which for most people is the point.

Choose it if you have used a sauna before and liked it; if you want the ritual rather than a treatment; if more than one person will use it. Both our standard cabins are traditional, with Harvia heaters sized to the cabin.

Infrared

Infrared panels emit radiant heat that warms your body without heating the air much. You sit at 50-ish degrees and sweat heavily anyway, because the heat is going into you rather than around you.

Sessions are longer and gentler — forty minutes at 50°C rather than fifteen at 90°C. There is no steam, no ladle, no stones. Some people find it more tolerable and more repeatable; others find it quiet and a bit clinical, like sitting in front of a warm wall. Both reactions are common and neither is wrong.

Choose it if high air temperature is uncomfortable for you; if you want short warm-up and low running cost; if it is mainly for one person after training.

Hybrid

One cabin, both systems: the Harvia heater and stones for a traditional session, infrared panels for a gentler one. You decide on the day.

It costs more than either alone and it is the honest answer for a household that disagrees — one person who wants 90°C and water on the stones, another who finds that unbearable. We add infrared panels to our cabins on request.

Choose it if more than one person will use it and they want different things; or if you genuinely cannot decide, which is a legitimate reason.

What about running costs?

Infrared draws less: lower power, shorter warm-up, no long hold at high temperature. But the gap is smaller than people assume, because a traditional sauna's heater cycles rather than running flat out, and because in Dubai a cabin that starts at 40°C has less climbing to do.

We worked a traditional session through the DEWA slabs in what a home sauna costs to run: about AED 1.90 a session for a two-person cabin. Infrared would land under that. Neither number should decide a purchase of this size.

What about the health claims?

Careful here. There is a real and growing body of research on regular sauna use, most of it conducted on traditional Finnish saunas, which is worth knowing when a seller quotes that research at you about an infrared cabin. The two are not interchangeable in the evidence.

We build saunas; we are not the right people to tell you what one will do for your blood pressure. If you have a heart condition, are pregnant, or are on medication, ask a doctor rather than a sauna company — including this one.

The practical answer

If you have used a sauna before and know you like it, get a traditional one. If high heat has always been too much for you, infrared will get used and a traditional cabin will not. If the household is split, build the hybrid and stop arguing.

And whichever you choose, the thing that decides whether it gets used is not the technology. It is whether it is ready when you want it — which is why the WiFi panel matters more than the brochure suggests.
