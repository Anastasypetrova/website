What the research says about saunas and health

There is a serious body of research here. There is also a great deal of nonsense wrapped around it. This separates the two.

Dubai, UAE · Updated November 2026

Most of what is known about saunas and long-term health comes from one place: eastern Finland, where researchers have followed the same population for decades in a country where the sauna is ordinary rather than a wellness purchase.

The Finnish cohort

The Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease Risk Factor Study followed 2,315 middle-aged men, aged 42 to 60, for around two decades. The sauna analysis, led by Jari Laukkanen and published in JAMA Internal Medicine in 2015, found that men who used a sauna more often had lower rates of sudden cardiac death, fatal coronary heart disease, fatal cardiovascular disease and death from any cause.

The pattern was dose-dependent: four to seven sessions a week tracked with lower risk than two to three, which tracked with lower risk than once. Dose-response is one of the things that makes an association more interesting, because it is harder to explain away.

A 2018 follow-up in BMC Medicine extended the work to women and found that sauna frequency improved the prediction of long-term cardiovascular mortality risk beyond the standard risk factors. The whole body of work was summarised in Mayo Clinic Proceedings in 2018.

Three honest limits on that

This is the part that usually goes missing.

  • It is observational. Nobody randomised anyone to a sauna for twenty years. People who sauna four times a week in Finland may differ from people who do not in ways no statistical adjustment fully captures — they may be healthier to begin with, which is precisely why they can tolerate it.
  • It is largely one research group. A great deal of this literature comes from the same team working with the same cohort. That is not a criticism of the work; it is a reason independent replication matters and has not really happened.
  • The authors say so themselves. Their own papers call for randomised studies to establish the mechanism. They are more careful than the people quoting them.

What the randomised trials show

When you move from watching populations to assigning people, the picture gets patchier.

OutcomeWhat the trials found
Depressive symptomsSmall but consistent benefit — 7 trials, 363 people
Blood pressure, vascular functionMixed; some signal, small studies
CholesterolNo clear effect
Athletic performanceNo effect

The depression result is the most solid pooled finding in the whole area: a systematic review and meta-analysis of seven randomised trials covering 363 people found a small but statistically significant reduction in depressive symptoms (Hedges' g = 0.32, 95% CI 0.12–0.52), appearing within about two weeks. Small effect, real effect, modest sample.

On cardiometabolic markers, a 2025 meta-analysis of passive-heating randomised trials found a far less decisive picture than the cohort data suggests — several outcomes with no significant effect, and most trials small. And on performance, the 2025 review discussed in our recovery article found nothing.

What is measurably true during a session

Away from long-term outcomes, the short-term physiology is not in dispute. Heart rate rises into a range comparable with moderate exercise. Blood vessels dilate. Core temperature climbs by roughly a degree. You lose fluid through sweat, which is water and salt rather than toxins — the body has a liver and kidneys for that, and sweat is not how they work.

So what would an honest summary be?

Regular sauna use over years is associated with better cardiovascular outcomes in a large, long, well-conducted Finnish cohort, with a dose-response pattern. Randomised trials confirm a small benefit for mood, and do not yet confirm much else. The short-term physiological effects are real and well characterised. The large percentage claims circulating online are mostly untraceable.

That is a weaker claim than most sauna sites make and a stronger one than a sceptic would expect. It is also what the papers say.

Who should not, or should ask first

Pregnancy, heart conditions, unstable or very low blood pressure, medications affecting heat tolerance or hydration, and alcohol — which is the genuinely dangerous combination and the one implicated in most sauna-related incidents in Finland. Ask a doctor, not a sauna company. We build cabins; we are not qualified to tell you whether to sit in one.
